What Is a Melt Up?

In March 2000, John Chambers was on top of the world...

It was the height of the dot-com craze. And his company was leading the "Internet Revolution."

Chambers' results as CEO were remarkable... He even appeared on the cover of BusinessWeek in 1999:

In just five short years, the firm's revenue grew from $2 billion to $12 billion. That's annual growth of more than 40%.

Chambers had a real business at a time when most folks were chasing garbage business models wrapped in gimmicky Internet lingo.

Investors loved its consistent and unbelievably strong growth trend. So the company's stock soared, like every other Internet stock at the time. But this one was different...

It didn't rise simply because of the irrational boom that dominated that era. It was fueled by strong fundamentals. The company earned more than $12 billion in revenue and surpassed $2 billion in profit in 1999.

And of course, investors made a mint along the way... The stock skyrocketed 131% in 1999. And by March 2000 – when shares peaked – it was up roughly 50% more.

That's almost a 250% rise in just 15 months. And it made Chambers' company the world's largest, with a market value of roughly $500 billion.

If you haven't already figured it out, we're talking about Cisco Systems (Nasdaq: CSCO), the giant Internet-infrastructure business.

The company's routers, switches, and network equipment still power the Internet as we know it. And clearly, the "Internet Revolution" that Cisco promised investors came to fruition. Here's an excerpt from the company's 2000 annual report...

Over the next two decades, the Internet economy will bring about more dramatic changes in the way we work, live, play, and learn than we witnessed during the last 200 years of the Industrial Revolution. Cisco is well-positioned to help our customers survive the Internet Revolution by turning these changes into [a] competitive advantage.

Cisco hit the nail on the head. You can't say the same thing about most businesses from that period. But even still, it has been a rough ride for Cisco investors...

You see, Cisco was part of a phenomenon called a "Melt Up" – the explosive, euphoric surge of a bull market – right before everything collapses in a "Melt Down."

The Inevitable 'Changing of the Guard'

The long-term bull market of the 2010s never really ended.

At first glance, that seems to contradict reality. But once you understand the idea, it can help guide your investment decisions.

Here's what we mean...

What we think of as bull and bear markets happen all the time. They're typically short-term events, lasting roughly a year or so (with some exceptions).

However, if you step back and think long term, you can see that massive 10- to 20-year cycles are what really drive stock returns.

The long-term trend is key to knowing how to position yourself as an investor. But most folks have no idea these cycles even exist.

Here's how you can think of these short- and long-term bull and bear markets...

  • Cyclical: This is what happens over 12 to 18 months.
  • Secular: This is what happens over 12 to 18 years.

Secular trends tell us whether the market will be higher or lower in the longer term...

And despite the pain of 2022, we're still in the middle of a secular boom... one that should push stocks higher through the end of this decade.

Once you understand this story, the next question is clear...

What should I buy to take advantage of the continuing bull market?

If you believe stocks are about to soar hundreds of percent, then you probably realize that the right stocks could soar much higher.

Any investor would want to pinpoint those right stocks. But if you make the most obvious investment – the one that seems, at first, to make sense – it could cost you.

So what's that obvious, dangerous investment? Easy. It's buying the biggest stocks... the ones that led the market over the past decade.

However, if you study history, you'll see why this is a mistake. That's because a key feature of American capitalism is that there's a consistent "changing of the guard" among our largest companies.

The story of Cisco illustrates this phenomenon perfectly. Cisco fell from No. 1 to No. 30 on the list of U.S. companies, despite having a healthy and vibrant business.

Most folks would think that happened because the stock was too expensive back then. It was the dot-com boom, after all. And valuations were crazy across the board. So maybe you think Cisco was an individual case... just another stock that couldn't compete in the market.

Melt Up Driver No. 1: Artificial Intelligence

Countries and businesses alike are racing to master AI...

The starting gun fired in November 2022 when Silicon Valley research company OpenAI debuted a new chatbot, ChatGPT.

At its core, ChatGPT is a large language model ("LLM"). It has a vast amount of text data at its disposal.

Programmers give the data a system of "weights" – parameters that connect words to each other within the model. By tweaking these weights, programmers can get the model to "associate" words more strongly or weakly.

When you ask an LLM questions, it uses its weighted data to predict the word that is most likely to be correct. Then, it predicts a second word, and so on... until its response is complete.

This might sound like a clunky process when you break it down. But LLMs are capable of extremely human-like writing. They can analyze data quickly, and they can answer tricky questions like magic.

There's a good chance you already know what I'm talking about. ChatGPT reached 100 million active users in just two months. Today, it has more than 180 million users, with 600 million visits to its website each month.

The bull case for AI is obvious. It's a revolutionary product with proven, durable demand. And the market loves it...

Major computer-learning players like Alphabet (Nasdaq: GOOGL), Microsoft, and Meta Platforms (Nasdaq: META) are up between 65% and 200% over the past two years. And early AI adopter Palantir Technologies (NYSE: PLTR) is up almost 200%.

However, one of the "pure" AI plays is in the computer chips that can run LLMs. To see it, you only need to look at the company that has made big waves in this space...

Chipmaker Nvidia (Nasdaq: NVDA) has been the undisputed darling of the U.S. stock market, rising more than 700% over the same period. In June 2024, Nvidia became the second-largest publicly traded company in the U.S. with a market cap of more than $3 trillion.

These first AI movers are drawing a lot of attention, thanks to this rapid surge higher. But buying them today could be risky...

Although we don't agree with the narrative that AI is a "bubble," it's still very early in the life of the technology. So you don't want to buy in today with an expensive company like Nvidia.

To maximize your upside (and hedge against your downside), you want to find the "second level" AI plays... the companies that the market isn't in love with just yet.

As things stand now, a few promising second-level AI strategies are emerging...

The first play is to buy companies that will challenge the current "AI winners," but that the market isn't betting on yet.

The second play is to identify the companies in other industries that are revolutionizing their workflows with AI, particularly data-driven companies. With huge caches of information to feed into LLMs, they'll have the best shot to harness this technology's potential.

The last play is to buy companies that are helping to drive the AI boom in the background. These "picks and shovels" businesses are making the revolution possible... even if investors don't realize it yet.

AI-optimized companies are already the face of the emerging bull market... and we expect them to keep outperforming. That's why they're the first sector you should focus on during the coming Melt Up.

How Microsoft Became the Exception to the Rule

Staying on top requires constant work and innovation.

It's especially true in the U.S., where our largest companies undergo a consistent "changing of the guard." The strength to stay in the lead is rare. But there's always an exception to the rule...

Microsoft has defied history in the recent decades. It was a top-five stock in 2000... in 2007... at the market peak in 2022... and it's still a top-five stock today.

Now, the company has changed a lot along the way. That's typical, even for a dominant business... And of course, a dying company will also recalibrate and adjust its business model to try to save itself.

Still, unless you've followed Microsoft closely, you might not realize just how different it is today versus even a decade ago. And recently, it has flown somewhat under the radar for those lacking a keen investment eye.

Ask almost anyone what Microsoft does, and he'll probably give you one of two answers... "Windows" or "Office," or maybe both.

The reason is simple: These two products fueled the computer revolution. The Windows operating system dominated the market beginning in the 1980s, allowing personal computing to reach the masses. The Office suite helped users easily complete word-processing tasks and create spreadsheets, presentations, and a lot more.

Now everyone associates Microsoft with those two products. But importantly, Windows and Office do not explain why the company is the exception to the rule.

In fact, if these were Microsoft's only products, the company would look a lot more like a fallen giant than the World Dominator it has become. Instead, Microsoft has expanded its product offerings in a strategic way...

You see, Microsoft launched into the world of cloud computing about 15 years ago. The company first reported revenue for that business segment in 2009. It brought in $11 billion that fiscal year – only 19% of the company's total revenue.

Back then, cloud computing was a small slice of the larger pie. But now, it's the main reason why Microsoft has avoided falling from the top five U.S. companies for more than two decades.

The company's cloud business now makes up more than 40% of its revenue. And since 2015, that segment has grown more than 270%, while the overall business has grown just 78%.

This shift into cloud computing generated nearly $88 billion in revenue over the past year. That massive, fast-growing revenue maintained the company's leading status.

That growth also changed how investors value the company. We can see that reflected in the company's price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio – the most common valuation measure we look at – over the past 15 years...

Microsoft was consistently dirt-cheap in the years following the global financial crisis. The stock traded between 10 times and 15 times earnings from 2008 through 2014.

Then, the company's valuation took off...

Its P/E ratio roughly doubled from 2014 through 2018. That's partially because the overall market became more expensive in that period. But that wasn't the driving force for Microsoft...

You see, in that time, the stock made a clear shift. It went from begin cheaper than the overall market to begin more expensive. Take a look...

Microsoft was trading at around 10 times earnings at the start of 2013. The overall market was closer to a P/E of 15. But by the end of 2015, those numbers had flipped... Microsoft was trading at a P/E of around 22 compared with the overall market's P/E of 18. And that difference has only gotten more extreme.

The interesting thing is that 2015 was the year Microsoft's cloud-computing business became a growth engine for its overall business... Cloud computing accounted for 25% of the company's revenue that year. By 2019, the segment's revenue grew to more than 30% of the business. And it's more than 40% today.

The cloud-computing division injected this stodgy old company with new life. And that fast-growing segment came to dominate its activities.

In simple terms, Microsoft successfully reinvented itself.

Most big companies can't do that. And that's why Microsoft has been an exception to the changing of the guard.

Microsoft broke the mold... It maintained and grew its legacy businesses while also creating a brand-new cloud-computing business. That segment now accounts for most of its growth and much of its valuation. Without that kind of product innovation, Microsoft arguably wouldn't be the market leader it is today.

This Melt Up Ain't Dead Yet

Bull markets die when excitement reaches a fever pitch. When everyone is fully invested in stocks... and there's nobody left to buy... that's when the market hits its peak.

Most investors haven't been pouring money into the stock rally. They've been buying bonds and hoarding cash this year.

Eventually, folks will really get bullish. The fear of missing out ("FOMO") will kick in, and people will start flooding back into the stock market. That's when we'll see explosive returns.

We're simply not there today. And that means one thing... This boom ain't dead yet.

"Cash on the sidelines" is a bit of an inside joke in investment circles...

If you're looking for an excuse to buy in, you can always say there's cash on the sidelines. Once that cash pours into the market, prices will have to rise.

Of course, the basic reasoning makes sense. It's just that it's only part of the story.

The truth is, folks are always holding some amount of cash. There's always "cash on the sidelines." And the fact that it exists doesn't mean it will inevitably pour into the market.

You have to go one step further. It's not just about cash on the sidelines... It's about when there's too much cash.

That's rarely the case. But when it happens, that's when you can expect all that pent-up cash to eventually move into the markets.

We're in this situation right now. The easiest way to see this is by looking at the cash in money market accounts...

Money market assets have soared over the past couple of years. The surge has made headlines, and for good reason... Nearly $7 trillion is now sitting in money market funds, more than double the roughly $3 trillion we saw a few years ago.

However, the better way to see how much cash could leave the sidelines is by looking at retail money market accounts. That's money market cash held by individuals, not companies or institutions.

These folks tend to be less experienced. And that leads them to raise cash in tough times only to pump it back into stocks after a rebound is underway.

It's important to note that this figure trends higher over time. However, every time it has spiked over the past three decades, a good chunk of that excess cash ended up leaving money market funds... likely heading back into stocks.

Take the Emotions Out of Investing

When you buy a stock, one of two things will inevitably happen...

It will go up. Or it will go down.

In the beginning, it's really that simple. You buy a stock with just two possible outcomes.

But the truth is, things can get complicated really fast. And as that happens, it often leads to one of the biggest mistakes an investor can make: letting your emotions get in the way.

When you let your emotions take over, you often rush into decisions that you'll regret later... That's the case no matter which way a stock is moving.

One common and costly mistake is selling a big winner too early.

That happened to me with Netflix (Nasdaq: NFLX). When I owned it in my hedge fund, the stock rose sevenfold as Netflix's streaming business grew. But as the stock kept moving higher, I made a terrible mistake... I started to let my emotions take over.

After the stock doubled, I sold half of my shares. And when it doubled again, I sold some more. As the stock was doubling a third time, I exited the position altogether.

My analysis revealed that Netflix was trading at a 90% discount to its intrinsic value – in other words, a "10-cent dollar." So as the story played out even better than I could have hoped for, why was I selling it after it doubled? It was still a "20-cent dollar."

I thought I was conservatively managing risk and didn't want to be greedy. But I had it backward... To build a successful long-term track record, you must be greedy when the opportunity arises.

Finding a monster stock like Netflix only happens maybe once a decade – or even once in a lifetime. So it's critical that you make the maximum amount of money on such moonshots.

I should've made more than $100 million on Netflix for myself and my investors. Instead, I made less than $10 million. Of course, that's not terrible... But it was a costly mistake.

It's equally important to harness your emotions when a stock is running against you...

Take SodaStream, for example. Its machines help you turn regular tap water into sparkling water with the touch of a button.

I knew SodaStream had a great business model. The company sells something that people use over and over. And the carbon-dioxide bottles in its machines need to be replaced regularly. So SodaStream made something like an 80% profit margin doing so.

But the company had botched its marketing in the U.S. and was also relocating its main factory in Israel, so its sales and earnings were down. I patiently waited until the stock had been cut in half and bought a small position in 2014.

It turns out that I was much too early. The company continued to struggle, and the stock kept drifting lower and lower... for nearly two years!

Making the right decision in these situations is critical. Had I stumbled into a "value trap" that would never turn around – in which case I needed to sell? Or was the company still strong, with fixable problems – in which case I should buy more?

It was very painful losing so much money for so long. Emotionally, I wanted to sell and never think about this terrible investment again.

But I was able to set aside my emotions and focus my analysis on the fundamentals, which remained strong. I added to my position all the way to the bottom – and was well rewarded.

In early 2016, SodaStream's stock took off as I expected...

By the time I closed my funds in late 2017, it was up 5 times. And then PepsiCo (Nasdaq: PEP) bought the company a year later for 12 times the price from only two years earlier.

It can be challenging to figure out whether a stock is just hitting a few speed bumps, like SodaStream, or if it's doomed for good, like old-school film company Eastman Kodak (NYSE: KODK). But by following a simple three-step process, I realized that I should hold on to SodaStream...

1. First, assume the market is right and you're wrong.

You must begin with this mindset because it helps overcome the natural bias we all have to not want to admit a mistake.

You must respect the market. The hard truth is that most of the time it's right... and you're wrong. My experience with SodaStream is the exception, not the rule.

2. Then, you must figure out what you've missed and actively seek out disconfirming information.

Redo your work... But don't just rehash what you already did. That won't lead to any new conclusions. Instead, you must ask – and honestly and correctly answer – a series of key questions.

Have you made a research error? Are you possibly missing anything? Have you openly and carefully considered contrary arguments? Have you invented new reasons to own the stock (so-called "thesis drift")?

Many smart investors lost a lot of money owning Kodak's stock in the decade before it filed for bankruptcy in January 2012. It wasn't an unreasonable investment initially... The company had one of the strongest brands in the world, it generated robust cash flows, and its stock traded at a low multiple of earnings.

Sure, digital photography was a threat to Kodak's film business, but it seemed far off – and the company was making investments to compete in this space.

For most investors who lost money with Kodak, the mistake wasn't so much the initial purchase. Rather, it was failing to recognize that the film industry was rapidly begin obliterated and that Kodak was getting no traction in the digital arena. So its profits were destined to disappear.

The key is to tune out the noise and think clearly and rationally. Focus on the fundamentals... If the company's earnings rebound, its stock will as well.

3. Lastly, to make the right decision, you must pretend like you don't already own the stock.

It's so hard to make the right decision about a stock you've lost money on. The emotions are so powerful!

On one hand, you're probably telling yourself that if you liked it at the price you bought it, you should like it more now that it's cheaper. That may be true – but it could also be a value trap. No matter what, you must resist the temptation to double down again and again to try to make back your losses. Remember the old saying... "You don't have to make it back the same way you lost it."

On the other hand, your emotions are likely telling you to sell so that you don't have to suffer any more pain and never have to think about this terrible stock ever again.

There's also a powerful feeling of wanting to wait until it gets back to the price you bought it before selling.

You must resist all of these feelings! Emotions are deadly when it comes to investing...

I've found that it helps my thinking to pretend like I don't own the stock. I ask myself, "If I were 100% in cash today and building a portfolio from scratch, would I own this stock? And if so, what size of a position would I have?"

Doing nothing may be the best option, but you also must have the courage to admit a mistake and get out – or know that you haven't made a mistake and buy more.

If a stock is going against you, follow this simple three-step process. And if you wouldn't buy the stock if you were starting a portfolio from scratch, then you should sell it immediately.
